Choosing a venue

Lawn or banquet hall: how to choose

City Garden, Varanasi  ·  12 November 2026  ·  6 min read

Almost every family that walks into a wedding venue in Varanasi asks the same question within the first five minutes: should we do this on the lawn, or inside the hall? It sounds like a question about looks. It is really a question about weather, timings and how your guests will move.

Here is how the two actually differ, written from the side of the people who set the chairs out.

What an open lawn gives you

Space, mostly — and the kind of space that does not feel managed. A marriage lawn lets a baraat arrive the way a baraat wants to arrive, with the band loud and nobody worrying about a ceiling. It lets the mandap sit under actual sky. It lets five hundred people stand around a stage without anyone feeling pressed into a corner, and it lets the catering counters spread out far enough that the queue never becomes a line.

Lawns also photograph differently. Evening light on grass and marigold does something a tube light cannot, and your photographer will quietly thank you for it.

The cost of all that is exposure. An open venue is at the mercy of the month. From late November into January, Varanasi evenings get genuinely cold after nine, and an unheated lawn empties faster than you would expect. In the monsoon, one unseasonal shower rearranges your entire evening. Neither is a reason to avoid a lawn — it is a reason to have a plan for the roof.

What an indoor hall gives you

Certainty. A banquet hall does not care what the sky is doing. Air conditioning in May, a closed warm room in December, and a guaranteed answer to the question nobody wants to ask on the morning of the wedding.

Halls are also easier for the parts of a wedding that need people to sit, listen and hear. A sangeet with a sound system, a reception with speeches, a haldi where the families want to be close together — these all work better with walls. Sound in an open lawn disperses; indoors it holds.

The trade is intimacy for scale. A hall seats a defined number and that number is the number. You cannot absorb another eighty guests at seven in the evening the way a lawn can.

Why most families end up using both

In practice the choice is rarely either-or, and the venues worth looking at in Varanasi are the ones that give you both on the same address. A typical shape looks like this:

  • Haldi and mehendi — daytime, indoors or under shade, family only.
  • Sangeet — inside the hall, where the music and the dance floor behave.
  • Baraat and the wedding itself — the lawn, with the mandap in the open.
  • Dinner — counters on the lawn, with the hall standing by if the weather turns.
  • Reception — whichever one suits the season and the guest count.

That arrangement is the real reason to look for a venue that has both rather than one that has one. It is not about using every square foot. It is about not having to gamble.

The question to ask a venue is not “lawn or hall”. It is: if it rains at seven, what happens next, and who moves the chairs?

Three things to settle before you decide

The month

Work out your date before your venue style. A February wedding and a July wedding are different problems. December and January reward a hall or a heated lawn; October, February and March are when an open venue is at its best.

The real guest number

Not the invitation count — the number who will actually stand on the ground at nine in the evening. Families in Varanasi routinely see thirty per cent more people than the list says. A lawn forgives that. A seated hall does not.

Who is handling the switch

If the plan is lawn-with-a-hall-as-backup, ask explicitly how long the switch takes and whose staff do it. A good answer sounds like a number of minutes and a named person. A vague answer is the whole risk.
